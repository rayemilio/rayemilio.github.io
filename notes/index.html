Notes

Before software, I spent more than three decades immersed in music– not just creating and performing it, but studying it as a system.

Music is often described as emotional and expressive, but beneath that experience is structure: hierarchy, timing, pattern, tension, release, expectation, surprise. Music taught me that complex systems can remain elegant and understandable, that structure is what makes meaning possible.

Software turned out to be another language for the same ideas.

At both Berklee and MIT, I kept finding the same people: curious, creative tinkerers who treated systems as something to be explored rather than accepted. Different disciplines, same curiosity. Those were my people. They still are.

Over time I began noticing that the questions I was asking in music were appearing everywhere else. How does a system produce meaning? Not just how it functions mechanically, but why a particular arrangement of parts creates a particular experience.

Music theory asks that question through sound. Synthesis asks it through waveforms. Typography asks it through visual structure. Software asks it through interaction. Education asks it through learning. Community and nonprofit work ask it through people and communication. The domains change, but the underlying curiosity remains remarkably consistent.

Why does a particular design create clarity while another creates confusion? Why does one interface feel intuitive while another requires explanation? Why does one piece of music feel inevitable while another feels directionless? What separates systems that merely function, from systems that communicate?

Decades in education reinforced the same lesson music had already taught me: If it requires explanation, it isn’t finished. Every question a user asks is an opportunity to improve the design. Every point of confusion reveals something about the structure beneath it.

The name Objects on Purpose comes from this way of seeing the world. Whether designing software, building educational tools, composing music, or creating systems for efficiency, I’m interested in things whose structure has been chosen intentionally rather than inherited by default. Purposeful objects. Objects on purpose. The projects collected here may appear diverse at first glance, but they are all attempts to explore the same idea from different directions– how thoughtful structures create meaningful experiences.
